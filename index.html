Chapter 1 · LO 1What accounting actually is, and who reads it

Why we care Imagine you run a small shop. Money comes in, money goes out, you owe a supplier, a customer owes you. At the end of the month someone asks a simple question: did you make money? You genuinely cannot answer it from memory. Accounting exists to answer that question reliably — and to answer it in a way a bank, a tax office or an investor will accept.

Accounting is the process of keeping financial records — more precisely, a systematic process of identifying, recording, measuring, classifying, verifying, summarising and communicating financial information. It reveals the profit or loss for a given period, and the value and nature of a firm's assets, liabilities and owner's equity.

📌 The definition to memorise Accounting consists of three basic activities — it identifies, records, and communicates the economic events of an organisation to interested users.

Those three words are worth learning in order, because "state the three basic activities of accounting" is about as classic a short question as this subject has. Here's what each one means:

Figure 1 — the three activities. Note where bookkeeping sits.
⚠️ Common mix-up Bookkeeping is not accounting. The accounting process includes the bookkeeping function, but bookkeeping is only the recording part. Accounting also identifies what's worth recording and communicates the result. Examiners love this distinction.

Who reads all this?

Users split into two camps, and the split is simply are you inside the company or outside it?

Internal usersExternal users
Managers inside the business — marketing, human resources, finance, production (e.g. store manager, production supervisor) Everyone outside — investors, creditors, taxing authorities (e.g. the tax office), regulatory agencies (e.g. SEC), customers, labour unions, suppliers

Chapter 1 · LO 2The building blocks: ethics, principles, assumptions

Why we care If every company invented its own way of reporting profit, no two sets of accounts could ever be compared — and every company would quietly pick the method that flatters it most. So the profession agreed on a common rulebook.
📌 Definition Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) — standards that are generally accepted and universally practised. These standards indicate how to report economic events.

Learn that acronym expansion cold. "GAAP stands for ______" is exactly the kind of free mark this paper hands out.

The two measurement principles

When you put an asset on the books, at what number do you put it? There are two answers, and choosing between them is a genuine trade-off.

📌 Historical Cost Principle (also called the cost principle) dictates that companies record assets at their cost.
📌 Fair Value Principle states that assets and liabilities should be reported at fair value — the price received to sell an asset or settle a liability.
💡 Think of it like… You bought a flat for ৳50 lakh in 2015; today it's worth ৳90 lakh. Historical cost says put ৳50 lakh on the books — it's a hard, checkable fact backed by a receipt. Fair value says ৳90 lakh — far more useful for a decision today, but it's an estimate and someone has to be trusted to make it. That tension has a name in the slides: the choice relates to trade-offs between relevance and faithful representation.

The two assumptions

📌 Monetary Unit Assumption requires that companies include in the accounting records only transaction data that can be expressed in terms of money.

This is why the health of the CEO, or how loyal your customers feel, never appears in the accounts — real and important, but not measurable in taka.

📌 Economic Entity Assumption requires that the activities of the entity be kept separate and distinct from the activities of its owner and all other economic entities.

Your business's money is not your money. If you take cash out for a personal expense, that isn't a business expense — it's a drawing, which we'll meet shortly.

🎯 Exam tip Straight from the slides: "Combining the activities of Kellogg and General Mills would violate the ______" → the economic entity assumption. Two separate companies, two separate sets of books.

Chapter 1 · LO 3The accounting equation

Why we care Everything a business owns had to be paid for by somebody. Either a lender put up the money, or the owner did. That single sentence, written in symbols, is the whole framework accounting is built on.
The basic accounting equation Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity
Remember: “what you own = what you owe + what's yours.” It must always balance — every transaction touches at least two accounts to keep both sides equal.

The slides describe it as providing the underlying framework for recording and summarising economic events. Two consequences follow immediately: assets are claimed by either creditors or owners, and if a business is liquidated, the claims of creditors must be paid before ownership claims. Creditors get paid first — that's why liabilities sit ahead of equity in the equation.

Figure 2 — the basic accounting equation.

The three components, precisely

ComponentDefinition from the slidesExamples
AssetsResources a business owns. They provide future services or benefits.Cash, Supplies, Equipment, Accounts Receivable
LiabilitiesClaims against assets — debts and obligations. Owed to creditors (the party to whom money is owed).Accounts Payable, Notes Payable, Salaries and Wages Payable
Owner's EquityOwnership claim on total assets. Also called residual equity.Owner's Capital, Owner's Drawings
⚠️ Why "residual"? Rearrange the equation: Owner's Equity = Assets − Liabilities. The owner gets whatever is left over after the creditors are satisfied. That leftover is the "residual", and the term residual equity is exactly the kind of word a fill-in-the-blank targets.

The expanded equation — four things move owner's equity

Owner's equity isn't one lump. Two things push it up, two pull it down:

Expanded accounting equation Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Capital − Owner's Drawings + Revenues − Expenses
Increases owner's equity ↑Decreases owner's equity ↓
Investments by owner — the assets the owner puts into the business Drawings — an owner may withdraw cash or other assets for personal use
Revenues — result from business activities entered into for the purpose of earning income Expenses — the cost of assets consumed or services used in the process of earning revenue
Common sources of revenue: sales, fees, services, commissions, interest, dividends, royalties, rent Common expenses: salaries expense, rent expense, utilities expense, tax expense
🎯 Exam tip Those two lists of examples are prime fill-in-the-blank material — "name three common sources of revenue" is a gift if you've memorised the eight. Note that dividends appears as a source of revenue here (income the business receives from investments it holds).

Chapter 1 · LO 4Transaction analysis — the Softbyte story

📌 Definition Transactions are a business's economic events recorded by accountants. They may be external or internal. Not all activities represent transactions, and each transaction has a dual effect on the accounting equation.

That middle sentence matters. Before recording anything you apply one test:

📌 The recording criterion Is the financial position (assets, liabilities, or owner's equity) of the company changed? If yes → record it. If no → don't.
EventRecord?Why
Purchase a computerRecordAssets change (equipment up, cash down)
Discuss product design with a potential customerDon't recordNothing in the financial position moved — no money, no obligation
Pay rentRecordCash down, expense up

Softbyte: ten transactions, one running equation

Ray Neal starts a smartphone app development company called Softbyte on September 1, 2017. We'll walk all ten events. Watch how the equation never breaks — that's the point of the exercise.

✍️ The ten transactions
1 · Investment by owner. Ray invests $15,000 cash. → Cash +15,000, Owner's Capital +15,000. An asset and equity rise together.
2 · Purchase of equipment for cash. Buys computer equipment for $7,000 cash. → Cash −7,000, Equipment +7,000. One asset swapped for another — total assets unchanged.
3 · Purchase of supplies on credit. Buys $1,600 of headsets and accessories, to pay in October. → Supplies +1,600, Accounts Payable +1,600. Asset up, liability up.
4 · Services performed for cash. Receives $1,200 cash for app development. → Cash +1,200, Revenues +1,200.
5 · Purchase of advertising on credit. Receives a $250 bill from the Daily News, payment postponed. → Accounts Payable +250, Expenses +250 (equity down). The expense is recorded now, even though no cash moved.
6 · Services performed for cash and credit. Performs $3,500 of services: $1,500 cash now, $2,000 on account. → Cash +1,500, Accounts Receivable +2,000, Revenues +3,500.
7 · Payment of expenses. Pays September rent $600, salaries and wages $900, utilities $200 — $1,700 total. → Cash −1,700, Expenses +1,700.
8 · Payment of accounts payable. Pays the $250 Daily News bill. → Cash −250, Accounts Payable −250. No expense here — it was already recorded in transaction 5.
9 · Receipt of cash on account. Receives $600 from customers billed in transaction 6. → Cash +600, Accounts Receivable −600. No revenue here — it was already earned in transaction 6.
10 · Withdrawal of cash by owner. Ray withdraws $1,300 for personal use. → Cash −1,300, Owner's Drawings +1,300 (equity down). Not an expense — a drawing.

Chapter 2 · LO 1Accounts, debits and credits

Why we care The Chapter 1 table worked — but imagine it with 400 transactions and 60 columns. Unworkable. So we give every item its own little record, and invent a shorthand for "up" and "down" that works the same way everywhere. That shorthand is debits and credits.
📌 Definition An account is a record of increases and decreases in a specific asset, liability, owner's equity, revenue, or expense item. An account can be illustrated in a T-account form.
The two words that trip everyone Debit = "Left"  ·  Credit = "Right"
Remember: debit/credit are just left/right — not good/bad. Assets & Expenses rise on the debit (left); Liabilities, Equity & Revenue rise on the credit (right). Mnemonic DEALER: Dividends·Expenses·Assets go up on debit; Liabilities·Equity·Revenue go up on credit.
⚠️ Unlearn the everyday meaning Debit does not mean bad or decrease, and credit does not mean good or increase. They are purely positional: left side, right side. Nothing more. Your bank says "credited your account" because from their books your deposit is a liability they owe you.
📌 Double-entry system Each transaction must affect two or more accounts to keep the basic accounting equation in balance. Recording is done by debiting at least one account and crediting at least one other. DEBITS must equal CREDITS.

If debits exceed credits in an account, it has a debit balance; if credits exceed debits, a credit balance. Which one an account should have has a name:

📌 Normal balance The normal balance is on the increase side of the account.
Figure 4 — memorise this one card and Chapter 2 is mostly done.
💡 Think of it like… Owner's Capital is the "home team" on the credit side. Revenues help the owner, so they play on the same side as Capital — credit. Expenses and Drawings take value away from the owner, so they play on the opposite side — debit. The slides put it exactly this way: the effect of debits and credits on revenue accounts is the same as their effect on Owner's Capital, and expenses have the opposite effect.
🎯 Exam tip Two multiple-choice items sit on the slides. "Debits: ______" → increase assets and decrease liabilities. "Accounts that normally have debit balances are: ______" → assets, owner's drawing, and expenses. Note the trap option "assets, expenses, and revenues" — revenues are credit-normal.

Chapter 2 · LO 2The journal

📌 The four steps in the recording process 1. Analyse each transaction (evidence comes from business documents — a sales slip, a check, a bill). 2. Enter the transaction in a journal. 3. Transfer journal information to ledger accounts. (Then, later, prepare a trial balance.)
📌 The journal The book of original entry. Transactions are recorded in chronological order.

It makes three named contributions to the recording process — a textbook 3-mark short question:

  1. Discloses the complete effects of a transaction.
  2. Provides a chronological record of transactions.
  3. Helps to prevent or locate errors, because debit and credit amounts can be easily compared.
📌 Journalizing Entering transaction data in the journal.

Chapter 2 · LO 3The ledger and posting

Why we care The journal tells you the story in date order, but it can't answer "how much cash do we have?" without reading every page. The ledger reorganises the same information by account, so each balance is one glance away.
📌 The general ledger Contains all the asset, liability, and owner's equity accounts.
📌 Posting Transferring journal entries to the ledger accounts.
💡 Think of it like… The journal is your bank statement — everything in the order it happened. The ledger is a set of labelled envelopes, one per account, where you re-file each item so you can count any one category instantly. Posting is the act of moving each entry into its envelope. Same facts, different filing.
Figure 5 — the recording process, end to end.
🎯 Exam tip Straight from the slides: "Posting: ______" → transfers journal entries to ledger accounts. The wrong options are all reversals — "normally occurs before journalizing" (no, after), "transfers ledger data to the journal" (backwards), "is an optional step" (it isn't).

Two more named things

The standard form of account used in practice is the three-column form of account (debit, credit, and a running balance column) — the T-account is the teaching simplification. And the chart of accounts is the list of all account names and numbers a company uses.

📌 The three steps of transaction analysis 1. Determine what type of account is involved. 2. Determine what items increased or decreased and by how much. 3. Translate the increases and decreases into debits and credits.

Chapter 2 · LO 4The trial balance

📌 Definition A trial balance is a list of accounts and their balances at a given time, prepared to prove that total debits equal total credits.
⚠️ The most-asked idea in this chapter A trial balance that balances does not prove the books are correct. Learn these five limitations — "the trial balance may balance even when…" is a standing exam favourite:
  1. A transaction is not journalized.
  2. A correct journal entry is not posted.
  3. A journal entry is posted twice.
  4. Incorrect accounts are used in journalizing or posting.
  5. Offsetting errors are made in recording the amount of a transaction.
The pattern: any error that hits debits and credits equally stays invisible.

Presentation rules — free marks if you know them

Dollar signsUnderlining
Do not appear in journals or ledgers. Typically used only in the trial balance and the financial statements. Shown only for the first item in a column and for the total of that column. A single line is placed under the column of figures to be added or subtracted. Totals are double-underlined.

Part B — Short questions 5 marks

S1 State the three basic activities of accounting and what each does.short
Accounting identifies the economic events relevant to the business, records those events systematically in monetary terms, and communicates the resulting information to interested users through financial statements.
S2 Distinguish between bookkeeping and accounting.short
Bookkeeping is only the recording function — the systematic logging of transactions. Accounting is broader: it includes bookkeeping but also identifies which events should be recorded and communicates and interprets the results for users.
S3 Differentiate between internal and external users, giving two examples of each.short
Internal users are managers within the organisation who plan and control operations — e.g. the store manager, a production supervisor. External users are outside the organisation — e.g. investors, creditors, the SEC, customers.
S4 State the monetary unit assumption and give one thing it excludes.short
It requires that only transaction data expressible in terms of money be included in the accounting records. So a factor like employee morale or the health of the owner, however important, is excluded because it cannot be measured in money.
S5 Why is owner's equity called residual equity?short
Because the owner's claim ranks last. Rearranging the equation, Owner's Equity = Assets − Liabilities, so the owner receives whatever remains after all creditor claims are settled. On liquidation, creditors must be paid before owners.
S6 Name the three contributions the journal makes to the recording process.short
(1) It discloses the complete effects of a transaction in one place. (2) It provides a chronological record of all transactions. (3) It helps prevent or locate errors, because debit and credit amounts for each entry can be readily compared.
S7 Which accounts have a normal debit balance and which a normal credit balance?short
Normal debit: assets, expenses, owner's drawings. Normal credit: liabilities, owner's capital, revenues. The normal balance is always on the side that increases the account.
S8 A trial balance balances. Does that prove the ledger is correct? Give two reasons.short
No. It only proves total debits equal total credits. It will still balance if a transaction was never journalized, if a correct entry was never posted, if an entry was posted twice, if incorrect accounts were used, or if offsetting errors were made — any error affecting debits and credits equally stays hidden.
S9 Distinguish a simple entry from a compound entry.short
A simple entry involves exactly two accounts — one debit and one credit. A compound entry involves three or more accounts, for example a purchase part paid in cash and part on account.
S10 Explain the difference between an expense and a drawing.short
An expense is the cost of assets consumed or services used in the process of earning revenue, and it appears on the income statement. A drawing is the owner taking cash or other assets out of the business for personal use; it reduces owner's equity but is not an expense and never affects net income.

Part C — Long questions 10 marks

L1 State the accounting equation, define each component, and explain what the expanded equation adds.long
The equation: Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity.

Assets are resources a business owns which provide future services or benefits — cash, supplies, equipment, accounts receivable. Liabilities are claims against those assets: the debts and obligations owed to creditors, such as accounts payable, notes payable and salaries and wages payable. Owner's equity is the ownership claim on total assets, also called residual equity because owners rank behind creditors if the business is liquidated.

The expanded equation breaks owner's equity into its four moving parts:
Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Capital − Owner's Drawings + Revenues − Expenses.
Investments by the owner and revenues increase equity; drawings and expenses decrease it. This is what lets a single equation carry both the balance sheet and the income statement.

Why it matters: it provides the underlying framework for recording and summarising every economic event, and it must remain in balance after every transaction — which is exactly what the double-entry system enforces.

L3 Describe the four financial statements, what each reports, and how they connect.long
1 · Income statement — reports revenues and expenses for a period of time; lists revenues first, then expenses, and shows net income or net loss. It excludes owner investments and withdrawals.
2 · Owner's equity statement — reports the changes in owner's equity over the same period: beginning capital + investments + net income − drawings = ending capital.
3 · Balance sheet — reports assets, liabilities and owner's equity at a specific date; a snapshot. Assets are listed first, then liabilities and owner's equity, and total assets must equal total liabilities plus owner's equity.
4 · Statement of cash flows — reports cash receipts and payments for a period, answering where cash came from, what it was used for, and the change in the cash balance.

The chain: net income from the income statement is needed for the owner's equity statement; the ending owner's equity from that is needed for the balance sheet; and the balance sheet together with the income statement is needed to prepare the statement of cash flows.

Illustrated with Softbyte: net income $4,700 − $1,950 = $2,750; ending equity $0 + $15,000 + $2,750 − $1,300 = $16,450; balance sheet $1,600 + $16,450 = $18,050 = total assets. ✔
L4 Explain the double-entry system and the rules of debit and credit for all five account types, with an example of each.long
Under the double-entry system, every transaction affects two or more accounts, and is recorded by debiting at least one account and crediting at least one other. Total debits must always equal total credits — this is what keeps the accounting equation in balance. "Debit" simply means the left side of an account and "credit" the right; neither implies good or bad.

The normal balance of any account is on the side that increases it:
• Assets — increase by debit. e.g. receiving $66,000 cash → debit Cash.
• Liabilities — increase by credit. e.g. buying supplies on account → credit Accounts Payable.
• Owner's Capital — increase by credit. e.g. the owner invests → credit Owner's Capital.
• Revenues — increase by credit, following the same rules as capital, because revenue benefits the owner. e.g. services performed → credit Service Revenue.
• Expenses and Owner's Drawings — increase by debit, the opposite of capital, because both reduce owner's equity. e.g. paying rent → debit Rent Expense.
L5 What is a trial balance, why is it prepared, and what are its limitations?long
A trial balance is a list of all ledger accounts and their balances at a given date, with debit balances in one column and credit balances in another. It is prepared after posting and before the financial statements, and its purpose is to prove that total debits equal total credits — a first check on the mathematical accuracy of the ledger, and a convenient summary from which the statements are drawn.

Its limitations — the trial balance may still balance even when:
1. a transaction is not journalized at all;
2. a correct journal entry is not posted;
3. a journal entry is posted twice;
4. incorrect accounts are used in journalizing or posting;
5. offsetting errors are made in recording the amount of a transaction.

The common thread is that any error affecting debits and credits equally is invisible to the trial balance. So a balanced trial balance proves arithmetic, not correctness.
L6 Analyse the first five Softbyte transactions and show their effect on the accounting equation.long
1. Ray Neal invests $15,000 cash → Cash +15,000; Owner's Capital +15,000. Asset and equity rise together.
2. Purchases equipment for $7,000 cash → Equipment +7,000; Cash −7,000. One asset exchanged for another; total assets unchanged.
3. Purchases supplies for $1,600 on credit → Supplies +1,600; Accounts Payable +1,600. Asset up, liability up.
4. Performs services for $1,200 cash → Cash +1,200; Revenues +1,200 (equity up).
5. Receives a $250 advertising bill from the Daily News, unpaid → Accounts Payable +250; Expenses +250 (equity down). The expense is recorded when incurred, not when paid.

After each one, Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity still holds — that is the dual effect in action.
